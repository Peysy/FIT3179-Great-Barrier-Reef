3.3 The Hidden Environmental Cost of Reef Tourism

While tourism contributes to regional economies, recreational diving can also place physical pressure on coral reefs.

A 2026 study involving 732 divers in Indonesia and the Philippines found that many damaging interactions were unintentional or went unnoticed.

Source: Lin et al. (2026). Observations were collected in Indonesia and the Philippines, not the Great Barrier Reef.

3.4 When Human Activities Threaten the Reef

While the Great Barrier Reef supports a thriving tourism industry, activities involving vessels can also pose environmental risks. Vessel groundings occur when boats come into contact with the seabed or coral reefs, potentially damaging marine habitats.

Recreational vessels consistently recorded more groundings than commercial fishing and tourism vessels between 2019–20 and 2022–23. However, these figures represent reported incidents rather than the relative risk of each vessel type.

Source: Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority (2024), Outlook Report, Figure 5.16. Values approximately digitised from the published figure.

3.5 How Is Coral Cover Changing?

Coral cover is an important indicator of reef condition. Monitoring across the Great Barrier Reef reveals differences in coral cover between its northern, central and southern regions.

Between 2025 and 2026, average hard coral cover increased in the Northern and Central GBR, while the Southern GBR experienced a slight decline. These changes reflect multiple environmental influences and cannot be attributed solely to tourism.

Source: Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS), Annual Summary Report 2025–2026.

Beyond Tourism: Protecting the Great Barrier Reef

Tourism operators do more than bring visitors to the Great Barrier Reef. Through conservation initiatives, participating operators help monitor reef conditions, control coral predators and support coral restoration.

Explore how tourism contributes to protecting the ecosystem that makes these experiences possible.

01 — RESPONSIBLE TOURISM

Growing Commitment to Responsible Tourism

Protecting the Great Barrier Reef also involves encouraging environmentally responsible tourism. The Reef Authority recognises High Standard Tourism Operators that meet independently certified environmental standards. The number of recognised operators increased from 19 in 2004 to 72 in 2023, highlighting growing participation in responsible tourism practices.

02 — MONITOR

Keeping an Eye on the Reef

Tourism operators regularly visit reef sites, allowing them to contribute valuable observations about reef conditions. Through monitoring surveys, they help reef managers understand changes in coral health and identify potential threats.

Survey counts are divided by the number of months in each reporting period to make comparisons more meaningful. Missing data does not indicate zero activity.

03 — PROTECT

Responding to Coral Predators

Crown-of-thorns starfish and Drupella snails feed on coral. Participating tourism operators help manage these predators through targeted removal activities.

These figures represent reported predator removals, not the total predator population or direct improvements in reef health.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

The Future of the Great Barrier Reef

The Great Barrier Reef is more than a major tourism destination. It is a fragile marine ecosystem facing environmental pressures, including coral bleaching, tropical cyclones and coral predators.

While marine zoning helps manage human activities, tourism operators also contribute to conservation through reef monitoring, predator control and coral restoration. The growing number of High Standard Tourism Operators further highlights the importance of environmentally responsible tourism.

However, these efforts alone cannot eliminate the Reef's environmental threats. Long-term protection also requires addressing wider challenges such as climate change and declining water quality.
